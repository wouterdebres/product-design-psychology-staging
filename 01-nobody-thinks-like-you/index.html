Nobody Thinks Like You

How false consensus makes you design for yourself

Maybe you chose a layout because it felt clean. Picked a label because it seemed obvious. Cut a feature because you could not imagine anyone needing it. You called that instinct. Maybe experience, if you were being kind to yourself. A lot of the time it was just you designing for yourself, in a room full of people who are nothing like you.

That blind spot is normal. More experience does not fix it by itself, because your own judgment keeps slipping in and posing as evidence. Researchers have been writing about this for decades, and designers keep making the same mistake because from the inside it feels reasonable. Feels earned. I still catch myself doing this when a screen feels obvious too fast.

False consensus in action

In 1977, Lee Ross, David Greene, and Pamela House at Stanford ran a series of experiments on what they called the false consensus effect. They gave people ordinary, unremarkable scenarios, a speeding ticket, a class assignment, a TV commercial, and asked them to predict what most other people would do. Then they asked what the participants themselves would do. Same pattern every time.

Social observers tend to perceive a ‘false consensus’ with respect to the relative commonness of their own responses.

— Ross, Greene & House

In plain terms, you assume most people would do what you would do. That is the move. When they do not, you start treating them like the exception.

That second part matters just as much. Ross and his colleagues did not only find that people overestimate agreement. They also found that participants judged people who chose differently as having more extreme, more revealing personality traits. The person who pays the speeding ticket assumes most people would pay it, then starts building a story about the person who contests it in court. They must love conflict, or feel paranoid about authority, or have something to prove. Same logic in reverse. Anyone who contests it thinks you are soft for not fighting back.

Both groups are doing the same thing. They start with their own choice, treat it as normal, then explain away anyone who does something else.

You see the same pattern in design reviews all the time. Someone builds a navigation pattern that makes sense to them, then describes users who cannot find their way around as “not our target user” or “low tech literacy.” Someone writes UI copy that feels obvious and decides people who misread it were not paying attention. I have seen teams do this in the room while the evidence sat right there in front of them. One person misses the menu label in a test and the room starts explaining the person instead of the label. Stop right there. That is the bias happening in real time, and it pushes you toward the wrong call while giving you a nice excuse for why the people who struggle do not count.

The brain does this because it has to start somewhere when it guesses what other people are like. Your own preferences, habits, and reactions are the nearest data you have. You have lived them, so they feel solid. So when your brain tries to estimate what is normal, it reaches for the closest example it can find, you, and starts there.

In product work, this gets worse fast. You use your own product with your own knowledge, your own patience, your own tolerance for ambiguity. You know where everything is because you put it there. You understand the terms because you wrote them. The flow feels smooth because it fits how you think. That is why the mistake feels like confidence. Really it is just familiarity getting mistaken for proof.

What this costs

In 2011, Color launched with $41 million and a strange idea about photo sharing. You took photos on your phone and the app mixed them with photos from other people nearby. Same party. Same street. Same room. The idea was that people would want that. A live stream built from whoever happened to be around.

A lot of people opened it and did not get it. Not in some grand product sense. In the basic sense. What is this for. Why is this showing me other people’s photos. Why would I keep using it. The company changed the interface within a week.

Later, the founders said they had launched “a network you don’t know how to get good at” and that they had “threw a mountain at people.” That sounds about right. They asked people to understand a whole new behavior all at once. From inside the company it probably felt simpler than it looked from the outside, because the people building it already knew what the thing was supposed to be.

That is what happens. The people closest to the product can still see the point. Everyone else is still trying to work out what this thing is.

I have seen smaller versions of this in product work over and over. A label feels obvious because the team already knows the feature. A flow feels easy because the people reviewing it already know what comes next. Someone gets stuck in a test and the room starts explaining the user instead of the screen. That is usually where the trouble starts. Not with some huge strategic failure, with a room full of smart people trusting their own familiarity too much.

The hardest thing about false consensus is how normal it feels. Knowing about the bias does not protect you from it. Bosveld, Koomen, and van der Pligt found the same pattern in a different setting years later. Your brain keeps defaulting to similarity, and awareness helps a little, but it does not solve it.

The user you imagine

The shift is simple, not easy. Before you make a decision, name out loud whose preference you are acting on. Whose, exactly. Is this layout choice based on something you observed in a user, or on what you would want if you were using this product?

If you hesitate, that hesitation is the answer. Means you are pulling from your own head.

One test is worth running. After any design choice, ask: would someone who has never seen this product land here and see what I see? Not after a tour. Not on a second try. Right now. If you cannot say yes with evidence, you are leaning on your own experience again.

Pay attention to how you talk about users who struggle too. If your first reaction to a failed user test is to describe the participant as confused, distracted, or not representative, you are watching the bias happen in real time. The person who does not behave the way you expected is the data. Read that again. The data. A product that only works for people who think like you is a mirror with buttons on it.

Do not get me wrong. Your instincts are not worthless. Pattern recognition built from real observation matters. But pattern recognition built from your own preferences, dressed up over years as expertise, is still an assumption. Those are the dangerous ones, because they do not feel like assumptions at all.

The most dangerous thing in design is getting it wrong while feeling dead certain you got it right.
